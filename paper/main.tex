\title{Borromean trimers on the Bethe lattice}

\author{Alexei Vazquez}
\affiliation{Nodes \& Links Ltd, Cambridge, United Kingdom}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Three particles can be bound when no two of them are, the Borromean binding
of Efimov physics and halo nuclei. I show that this happens on the Bethe
lattice, the tree that random regular graphs become in the thermodynamic
limit, for three distinguishable particles with on-site attraction $U$. The
pair binds at $\Up=2t(K-1)/\sqrt{K}$ exactly, with $K$ the branching and
$t$ the hopping. The trimer binds at $\Ut<\Up$ for every $K\ge2$, with the
Borromean window $(\Up-\Ut)/\Up$ growing from $7\%$ at $K=2$ to $16\%$ at
$K=10$. Two identical fermions and a third particle of the same mass have no
trimer, so the effect requires three flavours. A single site too shallow to hold one particle holds three before it
holds two, at an attraction between $0.58$ and $0.70$ of the pair threshold
for every depth of the site, which places Borromean trions in the Lifshitz
tail of any disordered network. For three-flavour
attractive fermions on random graphs, the trion Fermi liquid therefore
reaches zero density and the colour superfluid needs a finite density to
compete.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\textit{Introduction.---}Two particles are the unit of the theory of
superconductivity. Three particles are the unit of Efimov physics: with a
resonant two-body interaction, three particles have a tower of bound states
even when no pair is bound~\cite{efimov1970,nielsen2001}, which has been
observed in ultracold caesium~\cite{kraemer2006} and in the helium
trimer~\cite{kunitski2015}. Bound triples without bound pairs, Borromean
states, are also known on lattices, where the band structure replaces the
resonance: on the cubic lattice three particles with on-site attraction bind
at an attraction below the pair threshold~\cite{mattis1984,mattis1986}.
In many-body physics the triple appears as the trion of three-flavour
attractive fermions, the cold-atom analogue of the baryon, which competes
with the colour superfluid of pairs~\cite{rapp2007,inaba2009,inaba2010}
and has a Cooper-problem analogue, the Cooper
triple~\cite{niemann2012,akagami2021,tajima2022}.

Random networks are the other place where the pair-versus-triple question
has consequences. A random regular graph is locally a tree, and in the
thermodynamic limit its local physics is that of the Bethe lattice of the
same degree, the structure on which the cavity method is
exact~\cite{abouchacra1973,vazquez2023pre}. The attractive Hubbard model has
been solved on it by dynamical mean-field
theory~\cite{keller2001,garg2005,toschi2005}, the disordered case by the
cavity method~\cite{ioffe2010,feigelman2010}, and the three-flavour case
without disorder~\cite{inaba2009,inaba2010}. Yet the few-body anchor of all
these phase diagrams, whether the trion or the pair is the first composite
to form as the attraction grows at zero density, is not known on a tree.
Here I compute it. I reduce the two- and three-body problems on the Bethe
lattice to their automorphism-invariant sectors, obtain the pair threshold
in closed form, the trimer threshold numerically, and repeat both for a
single deep site, the dilute limit of disorder.

\textit{Model.---}Consider the Bethe lattice of degree $z=K+1$, the infinite
tree in which every vertex has $K+1$ neighbours, with $K$ the branching.
Distinguishable particles $p=1,\dots,m$ hop between neighbouring vertices
with amplitude $-t$ and attract on site,
\begin{equation}
  H=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\sum_{p}
  \left(c^{\dagger}_{ip}c_{jp}+\text{h.c.}\right)
  -U\sum_{i}\sum_{p<q}n_{ip}n_{iq}
  +\sum_{i}\eps_{i}n_{i},
  \label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
where $n_{ip}=c^{\dagger}_{ip}c_{ip}$, $n_{i}=\sum_{p}n_{ip}$, and
$\eps_{i}$ is a site energy, zero everywhere except in the impurity problem
below. For three flavours of fermions the flavours are the labels $p$ and
the statistics plays no role. The single-particle spectrum of the tree is
the band $|E|\le2\sqrt{K}\,t$ with the Kesten--McKay density of
states~\cite{kesten1959,mckay1981}, so $m$ free particles have their
continuum edge at $-2m\sqrt{K}\,t$, and a composite of $m$ particles is
bound when its energy lies below that edge. I set $t=1$.

\textit{Invariant sector.---}On a lattice the few-body problem is solved at
zero total momentum. The tree has no momentum but it has a transitive
automorphism group, and the analogue is the sector of wavefunctions that
are constant on the orbits of that group. For two particles the orbit is
their distance $d$, with $n_{d}=(K+1)K^{d-1}$ configurations per particle at
$d\ge1$. For three particles the orbit is the triple $(a,b,c)$ of distances
from their median, the unique vertex that lies on all three geodesics, with
the particles at positive distance in distinct branches of the median; the
orbit has $n(a,b,c)=(K+1)^{(k)}K^{a+b+c-k}$ configurations per median, where
$k$ is the number of positive entries and $x^{(k)}$ is the falling
factorial. The Hamiltonian acts on orbit functions $f$ through a
non-symmetric matrix $M$ of hop counts that satisfies detailed balance with
respect to $n$, so $g=\sqrt{n}\,f$ obeys a symmetric problem with
kinetic elements $-t\sqrt{M_{ss'}M_{s's}}$. Square-summability with the
measure $n$ makes the relative motion normalisable while the centre of mass
stays uniform, as at zero momentum.

\textit{Pair.---}The two-body invariant sector is a half line in $d$ with
hopping $J=2\sqrt{K}\,t$ for $d\ge1$, hopping $J_{0}=2\sqrt{K+1}\,t$ on the
first link, and site energy $-U$ at $d=0$. The cavity element of the half
line at the band edge is $1/J$, and the bound state condition
$-U-E-J_{0}^{2}/J=0$ at $E=-2J$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \Up=2J-\frac{J_{0}^{2}}{J}=\frac{2(K-1)}{\sqrt{K}}\,t .
  \label{eq:U2}
\end{equation}
The pair energy for $U>\Up$ is $E_{2}=-J(\lambda+1/\lambda)$ with
$0<\lambda<1$ the root of $U=J(\lambda+1/\lambda)-J_{0}^{2}\lambda/J$. On
the line, $K=1$, the threshold vanishes as it must, and at large $U$,
$E_{2}=-U-4(K+1)t^{2}/U$, the on-site shift $-2(K+1)t^{2}/U$ plus the
energy of a pair that hops with amplitude $2t^{2}/U$ in the uniform mode of
the tree.

\textit{Trimer.---}The three-body sector has no closed form and I
diagonalise it with the orbits truncated at $a+b+c\le L$. The truncated
continuum lies above the true edge $-6\sqrt{K}\,t$, so the smallest $U$ at
which the ground energy drops below the edge is an upper bound on the
threshold $\Ut$ that decreases with $L$; it is already converged to the
bisection grid at $L=60$ and unchanged at $L=120$ and $180$. The reduced
model reproduces exact diagonalisation of three particles on a ring at
$K=1$ to eight digits, the closed form~\eqref{eq:U2} for the pair, and the
large-$U$ expansion $E_{3}=-3U-3(K+1)t^{2}/(2U)+O(t^{4}/U^{3})$.

Table~\ref{tab:thr} and Fig.~\ref{fig:bind} contain the main result. On
every tree with $K\ge2$ the trimer binds before the pair, $\Ut<\Up$, and the
Borromean window $(\Up-\Ut)/\Up$ widens with the branching, from $7\%$ at
$K=2$ to $16\%$ at $K=10$. Above $\Up$ the trimer stays below the continuum
of a bound pair and a free particle, $E_{2}(U)-2\sqrt{K}\,t$, at every $U$
computed, so the trion is the ground composite for all $U>\Ut$. This is the
tree version of the cubic-lattice result of Mattis and
Rudin~\cite{mattis1984}, with the coordination in the role of the
dimension: the window does not close as the tree gets bushier.

\textit{Two identical fermions.---}The result depends on the three
particles being distinguishable. In the sector antisymmetric under the
exchange of particles 1 and 2, with attraction only between unlike
particles, the ground energy never drops below the pair-plus-particle
continuum, for all $U\le14t$ at $K=2$ and $K=3$, and the gap to it closes
only slowly. Two equal-mass fermions and a third particle do not form a
trimer on the tree, as in free space, where a mass ratio above $8.2$ is
needed~\cite{kartavtsev2007}. The Borromean trimer of the tree is a
three-flavour object.

\textit{One deep site.---}Disorder enters at zero density through its
rare sites. Consider a single vertex, the root, with energy $\eps_{0}<0$ on
an otherwise clean tree. A static impurity is a fourth particle that does
not hop, and the invariant sector becomes the sector of the root-preserving
automorphisms, which relabel the children of every vertex independently. A
configuration of $m$ mobile particles is reduced to canonical form by
relabelling branches in order of first use along the particle paths, its
orbit measure is a product of falling factorials over the vertices of the
tree spanned by the root and the particles, and the rest of the
construction is as before. Every state in this sector is square-summable,
so the bound states are ordinary ones, localised at the impurity. The
thresholds follow from the Birman--Schwinger principle: with $V$ the
diagonal operator that counts coincident pairs, a bound state below the
edge $E$ exists when $U\lambda_{\max}\ge1$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the
largest eigenvalue of $V^{1/2}(H_{0}-E)^{-1}V^{1/2}$ and $H_{0}$ is the
Hamiltonian at $U=0$; the threshold is $1/\lambda_{\max}$ at the edge,
one eigenvalue problem instead of a bisection, and it reproduces the
bisection values of Table~\ref{tab:thr} to the bisection grid. One
particle binds to the root when $|\eps_{0}|>\eps_{c}=(K-1)t/\sqrt{K}$, from
$\eps_{0}-E-(K+1)t^{2}\mathcal{G}(E)=0$ with $\mathcal{G}$ the cavity
resolvent of the clean tree at the band edge, and the reduced model
reproduces this threshold and the impurity energy.

For $-\eps_{c}<\eps_{0}<0$ the site is too shallow to hold one particle,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:imp} shows the attraction it needs to hold two or three.
Both thresholds fall from their $\eps_{0}=0$ values to zero at
$\eps_{0}=-\eps_{c}$, and the trimer threshold is the lower one at every
depth: $\Ut/\Up$ runs from $0.70$ at $\eps_{0}=0$ through a minimum of
$0.60$ near $0.8\,\eps_{c}$ and back to $0.70$ at $0.99\,\eps_{c}$ for
$K=2$, and from $0.67$ through $0.58$ to $0.67$ for $K=3$. The Borromean
window at a shallow site is three to four times wider than on the uniform
tree. The $\eps_{0}\to0$ limit is the square-summable sector of the clean
tree, and its thresholds, $\Up=1.97\,t$ and $\Ut=1.38\,t$ at $K=2$,
$3.26\,t$ and $2.17\,t$ at $K=3$ (truncation $L=60$, still drifting down
by $0.01\,t$ from $L=40$), lie above those of Table~\ref{tab:thr}. The
reason is the one anticipated: a composite localised around a point pays
the band bottom of the tree for its centre of mass, the uniform sector pays
the lower Perron energy. The ordering is the same in both sectors and the
window is wider in the localised one. A site whose energy lies in the
Lifshitz tail of a disordered network therefore holds a trion at an
attraction where it holds neither one particle nor a pair.

\textit{Discussion.---}The model is realised, with its three flavours,
by $^{6}$Li: its three lowest hyperfine states have pairwise attractions
that are tuned together by a magnetic field, the mixture is stable enough
to be loaded into optical lattices, and its three-body physics has been
measured through recombination
losses~\cite{ottenstein2008,huckans2009,williams2009}. An optical lattice
with a speckle or quasi-periodic potential adds the site
disorder~\cite{billy2008,roati2008}. In that setting the results say the
following. As the attraction is ramped up at low filling, the first
composites to appear are trions on the deepest sites, at an attraction that
is $0.6$ of the one at which those same sites bind a pair, and the ratio is
the same for coordination $3$ and $4$; the onset of three-body loss, or of
a trion line in radio-frequency spectroscopy, precedes the onset of pairing
by a factor that does not depend on the details of the lattice. Because a
site holds a trion before it holds a pair, the low-filling state of the
disordered three-component lattice gas is a dilute liquid of localised
trions rather than of localised pairs, which is the zero-density end of the
trionic phase found at strong coupling in the clean
system~\cite{rapp2007,inaba2010}. The colour superfluid, which at finite
density is an instability of the Fermi sea at any attraction, has to enter
the phase diagram from finite density, and its boundary with the trion
phase ends at $\Ut$, not at $\Up$, on the zero-density axis. The
three-body loss that limits these experiments is, in this light, the
signature of the phase rather than an obstacle to it.

The mechanism is the one that makes light clusters in warm dilute nuclear
matter Borromean in the medium: there the deuteron dissolves at a lower
density than the triton and the helion because Pauli blocking removes the
weakly bound pair first~\cite{typel2010}, so between the two Mott densities
three nucleons bind and two do not. On the tree the band edge does what
Pauli blocking does in the nucleus, and the reduced problem suggests where
the advantage of the triple lies: the hops that change its shape at the
median are enhanced by the branch count, as the first hop of the pair is
in $J_{0}$, but they act in three relative directions instead of one.

For electrons the results are a selection rule. Two identical fermions and
a third particle of the same mass do not bind on the tree, so the
negative-$U$ centres of amorphous semiconductors~\cite{anderson1975}, which
on the continuous random network~\cite{zachariasen1932,weaire1971} bind
spin-paired electrons and explain the absence of a spin
resonance~\cite{kastner1976}, do not by themselves produce Borromean
three-electron centres. They can do so only where a third flavour is
available, an orbital or valley degeneracy, as with the lone-pair $p$
orbitals of the chalcogenides, or a heavy-light mass imbalance, and then
the prediction is a population of odd-electron, spin-carrying centres at
the shallow sites of the Lifshitz tail, present at a coupling where the
same sites hold no pair. Which amorphous semiconductors show an
odd-electron resonance that grows with disorder is then a question about
their orbital degeneracy.

For networks the trion is a dynamically generated higher-order
interaction. The Hamiltonian is pairwise, yet the bound object is a triple
that exists where no pair does, a hyperedge without its
faces~\cite{vazquez2023pre,battiston2020}, and the tree shows that
pairwise couplings on a branching structure generate such objects on
their own. Where a network model infers interactions from pairwise
statistics, this is the kind of structure it cannot see.

Two remarks bound the claim. The uniform sector places the centre of mass
of a bound composite in the Perron mode of the tree, whose energy lies
below the square-summable band bottom of the composite; it is what a
condensate of pairs occupies on a random graph and what the thermodynamic
limit of a random regular graph gives once its rare short loops are
ignored, and both thresholds in Table~\ref{tab:thr} are taken in the same
sector. The impurity problem has no such subtlety, and the ordering
$\Ut<\Up$ holds there with a wider margin. Second, the effect is a lattice
effect in the sense of Mattis~\cite{mattis1986}, a consequence of the band
edge rather than of a resonance, and there is no Efimov tower: the return
probability on a tree decays exponentially, so its effective dimension is
infinite and outside the Efimov window $2.3<d<3.8$~\cite{nielsen2001}.

In conclusion, on the Bethe lattice three distinguishable particles bind
before two, in a window that widens with the degree, and a shallow site
holds three before it holds two. The next step is the three-colour cavity
calculation at finite density and disorder, for which these thresholds are
the zero-density boundary conditions.
